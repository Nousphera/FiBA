On the Effectiveness of Invisible Backdoor Attacks in Federated Learning

Understanding invisible backdoors in collaborative learning

Mattjis Marinus · Vasileios Tsouvalas · Nirvana Meratnia

PaperCodePoster

At a glance

Test how visually subtle backdoors survive federated training. FiBA adapts invisible trigger families to a shared benchmark and compares their success, clean-model utility and detectability. A separate LSB experiment asks whether placing a trigger in low-activation image regions changes that tradeoff.

What this adds

  • 01

    A federated comparison: evaluate LSB, WaNet, Encoder and LIRA attacks alongside visible BadNet under controlled client participation.

Abstract

Federated Learning (FL) enables collaborative training of machine learning models across multiple devices, while preserving data privacy. However, it also introduces vulnerabilities to backdoor attacks, where malicious updates can corrupt the global model. This work focuses on the largely unexplored domain of Federated invisible Backdoor Attacks (FiBAs), which use visually indistinguishable triggers to manipulate model behavior without being detected. We investigate the feasibility and effectiveness of these attacks, considering the unique challenges posed by FL, such as limited local training time and the need for model update aggregation. Our study presents a comprehensive evaluation of the attack success rate (ASR) of invisible BAs under various settings and defenses. Based on our observations, we propose a backdoor trigger hiding technique based on low model attention regions to improve attack resilience in federated settings. Our findings provide critical insights into the optimization of invisible BAs in FL and highlights the need for robust defense mechanisms to safeguard FL systems.

How it works

FiBA

01

Adapt attacks to a federated benchmark

Use ResNet-18 on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 with nonoverlapping, non-IID client data. Malicious clients learn a common target from poisoned samples while honest clients train normally. Coordinated attackers share the required trigger string, warping field, encoder or pretraining resources for their attack family.

Sections 3.1 and 4, PDF pages 5–9. Main settings: 10 clients, 100 rounds, five local epochs, Dir(0.5) and poison rate 0.5. This is an evaluation study, not a new defense.

02

Compare one aggregation rule at a time

The defense grid compares FedAvg, Median, Trimmed Mean, FoolsGold, KRUM and Multi-KRUM under fixed attack conditions. Each rule can change both backdoor success and clean accuracy. Model replacement and distributed triggers are additional probes with their own assumptions.

Table 2 and Figures 3–6, PDF pages 11–14. The defense grid has BadNet, LSB, WaNet and Encoder rows; it does not include LIRA or report a PCA column.

03

Keep the evaluation axes separate

Attack success rate is the proportion of poisoned non-target test samples classified as the target. Clean accuracy measures ordinary utility; LPIPS and Neural Cleanse anomaly scores measure different aspects of detectability. Reported ± values are retained exactly because the paper does not define their dispersion statistic or run count.

Section 4 and Tables 1–3, PDF pages 9–11 and 14. These measurements do not establish statistical significance or a universal failure of robust aggregation.

04

Test low-activation placement specifically for LSB

A RankMe-type embedding score checks convergence before SmoothGradCAM++ maps are accumulated. The experiment compares reported low-, high- and unmasked placement for LSB500 and LSB700. Concentrating changes can also alter trigger intensity, so the comparison does not isolate attention from every other factor.

Section 3.2, Algorithm 1, Table 3 and Figure 7, PDF pages 7–8 and 14–15. Prose and mask-ordering descriptions differ; placement labels are preserved as reported. WaNet and LIRA attention placement remain future work.

LSB placement and detectability

Takeaways

  • Attacker participation changes outcomes: every tested attack has higher mean success at 20% than at 10% attackers in Table 1. The size of the increase varies considerably by trigger family.
  • Attack suppression can cost clean utility: for LSB on CIFAR-10 with 10% attackers, Multi-KRUM reports 1.47±0.45% attack success and 69.67±1.89% clean accuracy, versus FedAvg’s 41.59±6.19% and 83.37±0.33%.
  • Low-activation placement is a tradeoff: LSB700 gains 19.80 percentage points of mean attack success over unmasked placement, while its anomaly score rises from 1.61 to 3.04 and LPIPS also increases. Improved success is not improved stealth on every measure.
Study scope & sources
Data and model
CIFAR-10/100 with ResNet-18 trained from scratch; Flower simulations with Dirichlet concentration 0.5.
Threat model
Attackers poison local training data and coordinate a target/trigger; model replacement additionally assumes knowledge of aggregation-related quantities.
Attacks and defenses
LSB, WaNet, Encoder, LIRA and visible BadNet; reported defense tables cover Median, Trimmed Mean, FoolsGold, KRUM and Multi-KRUM.
Additional probes
Distributed triggers, transient model replacement, and low/high attention LSB placement; LPIPS visual distortion and Neural Cleanse model-level detection.

Scope & limitations

  • The experiments use one architecture, two small image datasets, ten clients, and specified attacker fractions. They do not establish effectiveness across arbitrary deployments, attacker access, or defenses.
  • The paper names PCA among evaluated defenses, but its result table does not report a PCA column. LIRA is also absent from the defense table; do not imply coverage of every attack–defense pair.
  • Attention-based placement is evaluated for LSB, while adaptation to WaNet and LIRA is left for future work. Higher ASR is accompanied by higher Neural Cleanse scores rather than improved stealth on that metric.
  • The ± notation and number of repeated runs are not specified. The distributed-trigger caption also combines four trigger parts with a 10% attacker fraction among ten clients, leaving the exact distribution setup unclear.

Public TU/e accepted manuscript, 17 PDF pages including repository cover sheet; UbiSec 2024 paper, proceedings published 16 May 2025. ↗
