On the Effectiveness of Invisible Backdoor Attacks in Federated Learning

Understanding invisible backdoors in collaborative learning

Mattjis Marinus · Vasileios Tsouvalas · Nirvana Meratnia

PaperCodePoster
OverviewMethodResultsCitation
The challenge

Visually subtle backdoor triggers must survive limited local training and competing honest updates in federated learning.

The contribution

The study benchmarks invisible backdoors and common defenses in federated settings, then tests attention-based trigger placement and its detectability cost.

Method overview · Based on the paper

The idea

At a glance

A visually subtle trigger does not behave the same way in centralized and federated training. This study examines how limited local training, competing client updates, and server-side defenses affect invisible backdoor attacks, then investigates whether placing triggers in regions receiving little model attention changes attack success and detectability.

  • Visual subtlety, clean accuracy, attack success, and model-level detectability are different properties.
  • A defense that lowers attack success can still be unsuitable if it substantially harms ordinary predictions.
  • The evaluated low-attention placement exposes a success–detectability tradeoff, not an unconditional improvement in attack stealth.

  • Federated robustness
  • Model attention
Read the abstract

Federated Learning (FL) enables collaborative training of machine learning models across multiple devices, while preserving data privacy. However, it also introduces vulnerabilities to backdoor attacks, where malicious updates can corrupt the global model. This work focuses on the largely unexplored domain of Federated invisible Backdoor Attacks (FiBAs), which use visually indistinguishable triggers to manipulate model behavior without being detected. We investigate the feasibility and effectiveness of these attacks, considering the unique challenges posed by FL, such as limited local training time and the need for model update aggregation. Our study presents a comprehensive evaluation of the attack success rate (ASR) of invisible BAs under various settings and defenses. Based on our observations, we propose a backdoor trigger hiding technique based on low model attention regions to improve attack resilience in federated settings. Our findings provide critical insights into the optimization of invisible BAs in FL and highlights the need for robust defense mechanisms to safeguard FL systems.

The approach

How it works

  1. 01

    Evaluate invisible triggers

    The study compares several invisible backdoor families with a visible-trigger baseline under federated training. Evaluation measures clean accuracy, attack success, and perceptual similarity.

  2. 02

    Probe training and defenses

    Experiments vary attacker participation and test server-side defenses, including robust aggregation and behavior detection. Distributed triggers and model replacement are also examined.

  3. 03

    Study attention-based placement

    Activation maps identify regions receiving less model attention. The paper evaluates placing LSB triggers in these regions and checks both attack success and detection by Neural Cleanse.

Experiments & evidence

What the results show

Read attack success together with clean accuracy and detection scores; lower visual distortion does not imply that a model-level defense cannot detect an attack.

Data and model
CIFAR-10/100 with ResNet-18 trained from scratch; Flower simulations with Dirichlet concentration 0.5.
Threat model
Attackers poison local training data and coordinate a target/trigger; model replacement additionally assumes knowledge of aggregation-related quantities.
Attacks and defenses
LSB, WaNet, Encoder, LIRA and visible BadNet; reported defense tables cover Median, Trimmed Mean, FoolsGold, KRUM and Multi-KRUM.
Additional probes
Distributed triggers, transient model replacement, and low/high attention LSB placement; LPIPS visual distortion and Neural Cleanse model-level detection.

Low-attention placement raises mean ASR by 19.80 percentage points over the unmasked attack, but increases both visual distortion and the anomaly index beyond the discussed detection threshold.

The tested attention-placement evidence is limited to LSB500/700 on CIFAR-10. Fewer modified regions can also increase trigger intensity, so placement is not isolated from every other factor.

Scope & limitations

  • The experiments use one architecture, two small image datasets, ten clients, and specified attacker fractions. They do not establish effectiveness across arbitrary deployments, attacker access, or defenses.
  • The paper names PCA among evaluated defenses, but its result table does not report a PCA column. LIRA is also absent from the defense table; do not imply coverage of every attack–defense pair.
  • Attention-based placement is evaluated for LSB, while adaptation to WaNet and LIRA is left for future work. Higher ASR is accompanied by higher Neural Cleanse scores rather than improved stealth on that metric.
  • The ± notation and number of repeated runs are not specified. The distributed-trigger caption also combines four trigger parts with a 10% attacker fraction among ten clients, leaving the exact distribution setup unclear.
